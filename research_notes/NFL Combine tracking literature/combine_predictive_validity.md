# Predictive validity of NFL Combine testing and draft position for NFL performance

> **How these sources were checked.** In this session, WebFetch and direct API access were blocked: DNS failures for pubmed, ebi, arxiv and digitalcommons.uri.edu, and the proxy refused api.crossref.org. Each item was therefore verified through web-search result pages that point to real records (PubMed mirrors such as bvsalud and ngdc, BISp/LIDA sport databases, RePEc/IDEAS, university repositories). I did not read the full texts. Numbers given below come from abstracts or secondary summaries, and the notes say which. Each item is marked with one of these levels:
> - **[VERIFIED]**: authors, title, venue and year confirmed on a real bibliographic page.
> - **[PARTIAL]**: the paper exists, but some citation fields are unconfirmed.
> - **[UNVERIFIED]**: I could not confirm the paper.

## Q1. Annotated list of core papers: combine and draft vs. NFL outcomes

### Takeaway
The combine/JSCR literature reaches mixed conclusions. Early studies found combine tests predict *draft status*, especially for speed positions. Studies that predict *NFL on-field performance* mostly find weak or inconsistent links. The exception is sprint and short-acceleration measures for RBs. Newer position-by-position Spearman studies find at least one significant drill per position. The economics literature adds that draft position itself is a poor guide to value (Massey & Thaler) and that it distorts playing time (Keefer).

### Cited Findings

**Combine → draft status**
- **[VERIFIED] McGee, K.J. & Burkett, L.N. (2003).** "The National Football League Combine: A Reliable Predictor of Draft Status?" *J Strength Cond Res* 17(1):6–11.
  - Data: the 2000 combine (about 326 players).
  - Findings (from secondary summaries): combine results predicted draft status well for RB, WR and DB, and also predicted draft round for QB. They were ineffective for OL, DL and LB. The authors attribute this to those positions depending on speed and agility.
  - Secondary summaries differ slightly on details, so the specific numbers should be checked against the original.
  - Sources: [Tucker & Black, The Sport Journal](https://thesportjournal.org/article/the-national-football-league-combine-do-performance-measures-predict-draft-status-among-nfl-draftees/); [New Republic summary](https://newrepublic.com/article/87354/2011-nfl-mock-draft-combine)
- **[VERIFIED] Sierer, S.P., Battaglini, C.L., Mihalik, J.P., Shields, E.W., Tomasini, N.T. (2008).** "The National Football League Combine: performance differences between drafted and nondrafted players entering the 2004 and 2005 drafts." *J Strength Cond Res* 22(1):6–12. PMID 18296949.
  - Data: N = 321, grouped as skill, big skill and linemen. Method: Student t-tests.
  - Drafted skill players were better than nondrafted ones on the 40-yd, vertical jump, pro-agility and 3-cone.
  - Drafted big-skill players differed on the 40-yd and 3-cone.
  - Drafted linemen differed on the 40-yd, 225-lb bench and 3-cone.
  - Note that the 3-cone separates drafted from undrafted players in all three groups.
  - Sources: [MEDLINE record (BVS)](https://busqueda.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-18296949). Co-author list is from my knowledge of the paper; the record confirms the title, journal, issue and PMID.
- **[VERIFIED] Robbins, D.W. (2010).** "The National Football League (NFL) combine: does normalized data better predict performance in the NFL draft?" *J Strength Cond Res* 24(11):2888–2899. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181f927cc](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181f927cc).
  - Data: 1,155 drafted players, 2005–2009, 17 positions. Eight physical tests were each analyzed raw, ratio-normalized and allometrically normalized, and correlated with draft order.
  - Finding: combine performance has little association with draft success, normalized or not, and normalizing adds no predictive value.
  - The task brief cites this as "Robbins 2011". The verified year is 2010.
  - Sources: [BISp SURF record](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201104003890)
- **[PARTIAL] (likely Robbins, D.W., 2011).** "Positional physical characteristics of players drafted into the National Football League." *J Strength Cond Res* 25(10):2661–2667.
  - Data: 2005–2009, 15 positions. Provides positional profiles: linemen performed worse on sprint, jump and change-of-direction (COD) tests and better on upper-body strength.
  - The title is confirmed by a LIDA record. The authorship is not confirmed in the search results.
  - Sources: [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4023411?lng=en)
- **[UNVERIFIED] Robbins 2012** ("relative performance" / draft order). Searches found no such paper. It may be a mis-citation of the 2010 or 2011 papers.

**Combine → NFL performance**
- **[VERIFIED] Kuzmits, F.E. & Adams, A.J. (2008).** "The NFL combine: does it predict performance in the National Football League?" *J Strength Cond Res* 22(6):1721–1727. PMID 18841077.
  - Data: QB, RB and WR drafted 1999–2004.
  - Predictors: 10/20/40-yd dashes, bench, vertical, broad jump, 20- and 60-yd shuttles, 3-cone, and the Wonderlic.
  - Outcomes: draft order, 3 years of salary, games played, and position-specific stats.
  - Method: Pearson correlations.
  - Finding: "no consistent statistical relationship" between combine tests and NFL performance, except sprint tests for RBs. The authors recommend psychological tests and using college performance as a job-sample test.
  - Critique: the samples are small (only about 10 QBs drafted per year), and the reliance on significance tests rather than effect sizes has been questioned ([Park, Berkeley undergraduate thesis](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/Research/Ugrad/Paul_Park.pdf)).
  - Volume/pages are from my knowledge of the paper. The title, journal and PMID were confirmed via a PubMed mirror.
  - Sources: [PubMed mirror (NGDC OpenLB, PMID 18841077)](https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/openlb/publication/OLB-PM-18841077)
- **[PARTIAL] Teramoto, M., Cross, C.L., Willick, S.E. (2016).** "Predictive value of National Football League Scouting Combine on future performance of running backs and wide receivers." *J Strength Cond Res* (2016; volume/pages 30(5):1379–1390 unconfirmed).
  - Data: RBs and WRs at the 2000–2009 combines, with NFL performance through 2013.
  - Findings (secondary summary):
    - RB: the **10-yd split** was the strongest single predictor of career rushing yards per carry.
    - WR: **height and vertical jump** were the strongest predictors of yards per reception.
  - Limitations: the study could not control for OL or QB quality or for how players were used.
  - The title and findings were confirmed via search summaries. Co-authors and volume/pages are not verified.
  - Source: search summary that cites the paper (via [arXiv 2303.05774](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05774) / [NASE blog](https://www.naseinc.com/blog/predictors-of-performance-in-pro-football/))
- **[VERIFIED] Vincent, L.M., Blissmer, B.J., Hatfield, D.L. (2019).** "National Scouting Combine scores as performance predictors in the National Football League." *J Strength Cond Res* 33(1):104–111.
  - Data: combine participants 2005–2010 who went on to play in the NFL. Sample sizes: QB 44, RB 82, WR 116, LB 139, DE 59, DT 72.
  - Finding: at least one combine measure correlated significantly with on-field success at every position. The authors call the tests "modest" predictors.
  - Effect size (press quote, not from the paper): Hatfield said the combine "can predict about 20-25 percent" of future success.
  - Sources: [URI DigitalCommons record](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/89); [Inside Science article](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/how-much-does-the-combine-reveal-about-future-nfl-players)
- **[PARTIAL] Anonymous in the search results (2020).** "National Football League scouting combine tests correlated to National Football League player performance." *J Strength Cond Res* (2020; 34(5):1317–1329 per one search summary, unconfirmed).
  - Data: all positions (e.g., QB n = 107, FB n = 31, RB n = 228).
  - Method: Spearman rank-order correlations.
  - Finding: every offensive and defensive position had at least one combine test correlated with NFL performance.
  - Authors not confirmed.
  - Source: [LIDA record 4062155](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4062155?lng=de)
- **[PARTIAL] Cook, Ryan, Snarr, Rossi (2020).** "The relationship between the National Football League Scouting Combine and game performance over a 5-year period." *J Strength Cond Res* 34(9):2492–2499 (per a secondary citation).
  - Finding: the combine lacks predictive ability for first-year game performance.
  - Source: [LIDA record 4064265](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4064265?lng=en)
- **[PARTIAL] Hedlund, D.P. (St. John's University), around 2018.** Analysis of 15 years of combine data comparing All-Pro, Pro Bowl and other players.
  - Finding: the average combine score is ordered All-Pro > Pro Bowl > neither.
  - The exact paper (probably JSCR, "Performance of future elite players at the NFL Scouting Combine") was **not verified**. Only press coverage was found.
  - Source: [Inside Science, Mar 2019](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/how-much-does-the-combine-reveal-about-future-nfl-players)
- **[VERIFIED] Mulholland, J. & Jensen, S.T. (2014).** "Predicting the draft and career success of tight ends in the National Football League." *J Quantitative Analysis in Sports* 10(4). DOI 10.1515/jqas-2013-0134.
  - Method: linear regression and recursive-partitioning trees on pre-draft data (college stats, combine, size).
  - Findings: the predictors of draft order differ from the predictors of career success. After adjusting for salary cost, size (BMI, weight, height) is **over-emphasized** in the draft.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/bpj/jqsprt/v10y2014i4p16n2.html); [Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/47937)
- **[VERIFIED] Berri, D.J. & Simmons, R. (2011).** "Catching a draft: on the process of selecting quarterbacks in the National Football League amateur draft." *J Productivity Analysis* 35(1). Pages are 37–49 per RePEc and 35–47 per Lancaster.
  - Findings: QB draft position correlates only weakly with later NFL performance. Many factors that raise draft position (including combine measures) do not predict performance.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jproda/v35y2011i1p37-49.html); [Lancaster ePrints](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/45327)
- **[PARTIAL] "NFL Career Success as Predicted by NFL Scouting Combine"** (arXiv 2303.05774, 2023). It argues that combine metrics influence draft position but do not predict later success. Authors not captured.
  - Source: [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05774)
- **[PARTIAL] Ryan, G.A., Harvey, K., Campbell, E., Shoebridge, M., Overby, L., Sauer, J., Herron, R.L.** "Relationship between NFL Combine measurables and playing time in the 2024 NFL rookie class." *The Sport Journal*. Findings not retrieved.
  - Source: [The Sport Journal](https://thesportjournal.org/article/relationship-between-the-national-football-league-nfl-combine-measurables-and-playing-time-in-the-2024-nfl-rookie-class/)

**Draft value (economics)**
- **[VERIFIED] Massey, C. & Thaler, R.H. (2013).** "The Loser's Curse: Decision Making and Market Efficiency in the National Football League Draft." *Management Science* 59(7):1479–1495. DOI [10.1287/mnsc.1120.1657](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1120.1657).
  - Finding: top picks are significantly overvalued relative to the surplus value they produce (performance value minus salary), which is inconsistent with rational expectations and efficient markets.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v59y2013i7p1479-1495.html); [CMU event page](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/statistics-datascience/cmsac/events/nfl-draft-event.html)
  - Critique: a 2024 arXiv paper ("The Loser's Curse and the Critical Role of the Utility Function", 2411.10400) argues that the conclusion depends on assuming GMs maximize expected surplus value. Source: [arXiv 2411.10400](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10400v2)

### Inferences
- The literature separates into two strands that should not be conflated:
  - **Predicting draft status.** Combine tests have moderate predictive power here, especially for speed positions.
  - **Predicting NFL performance.** Results are weak and inconsistent, and depend on the outcome metric. Using draft order as an "outcome" mainly measures what scouts believe.
- Most JSCR studies:
  - use bivariate Pearson or Spearman correlations;
  - test many drill × position × outcome combinations without multiplicity correction;
  - use small position samples;
  - rely on box-score outcomes such as games played or yards per carry, which are confounded by team context and by draft capital.

### Gaps
- Exact correlation coefficients, R² values and table-level results by position for Vincent 2019, the 2020 Spearman paper and Teramoto 2016 could not be retrieved. Full text was not accessible because fetch tools were blocked.
- A Robbins 2012 "relative performance" paper and the exact Hedlund 2018 journal citation could not be verified.

## Q2. Which drills correlate with which positions; effect sizes; do agility drills add info beyond the 40?

### Takeaway
Speed and short acceleration (the 10-yd split and 40-yd dash) are the most consistent signals for RBs. Height and vertical jump matter for WR yards per catch. For linemen, the evidence for drills predicting the NFL is weakest. The 3-cone separates drafted from undrafted players in every position group (Sierer 2008), but I found no strong published evidence that agility drills add predictive information for NFL *performance* beyond the 40.

### Cited Findings
- **RB sprint tests** were the only consistent combine predictor of NFL performance in QB/RB/WR samples — [Kuzmits & Adams 2008](https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/openlb/publication/OLB-PM-18841077)
- **RB:** the 10-yd split was the strongest single predictor of career yards per carry. **WR:** height and vertical jump were the strongest predictors of yards per reception — Teramoto et al. 2016 (via [search summary / NASE](https://www.naseinc.com/blog/predictors-of-performance-in-pro-football/))
- **3-cone and the draft:** the 3-cone separated drafted from undrafted players in skill, big-skill and linemen groups. The pro-agility shuttle did so only for skill players — [Sierer et al. 2008](https://busqueda.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-18296949)
- **Vertical jump** was reportedly recommended for retention because it was independent of the other tests. Robbins also reportedly suggested the 10- and 20-yd splits in place of the 40. This comes from a blog summary of Robbins 2010, so it is lower confidence — [80percentmental blog](https://80percentmental.com/blog/0percentmental.com/2014/03/why-nfl-combine-results-for-jadeveon.html)
- **Linemen:** combine results were ineffective in predicting draft round for OL, DL and LB — McGee & Burkett 2003 (via [Tucker & Black](https://thesportjournal.org/article/the-national-football-league-combine-do-performance-measures-predict-draft-status-among-nfl-draftees/))
- **Magnitude of effects:** modest. "At least one" significant correlation at every position — [Vincent et al. 2019](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/89). Explains about 20–25% of success per author quote — [Inside Science](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/how-much-does-the-combine-reveal-about-future-nfl-players)
- **Size vs. production:** size is over-weighted relative to career value for TEs — [Mulholland & Jensen 2014](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/bpj/jqsprt/v10y2014i4p16n2.html)

### Inferences
- Combine drills are highly collinear: the 40, splits, jumps and agility drills all load on body mass and power. Any test of whether agility "adds beyond the 40" needs partial correlations or nested models with mass controlled. Most JSCR papers do not report these.
- The "20–25%" figure is a press quote. It should not be read as a pooled R².

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis or pooled effect size across studies.
- I found no peer-reviewed incremental-validity test of agility drills (3-cone, shuttle) over the 40 for NFL performance outcomes.

## Q3. Sensor/tracking data (Combine RFID, Next Gen Stats, max speed) for characterizing athleticism

### Takeaway
NFL Next Gen Stats runs a proprietary, position-specific XGBoost draft model whose athleticism score is on a 50–99 scale and is trained on combine data from 2003 onward. Its exact formula is not public. Peer-reviewed tracking work in the NFL is mostly descriptive (positional max velocity and acceleration demands), and Big Data Bowl entries explore speed and acceleration profiles. I found no peer-reviewed study linking Combine RFID splits to in-game NGS max speed or performance.

### Cited Findings
- **NGS Draft Model (2020):**
  - Position-specific models produce probabilities that are converted into 50–99 composite scores for athleticism, production, size and overall.
  - Trained on combine data back to 2003 and college stats back to 2005, with feature selection per position. The algorithm is XGBoost.
  - Uses "threshold" logic: a prospect needs to be fast or big *enough* for the position rather than the best at it.
  - Source: [NFL.com](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-draft-model-can-predict-prospects-pro-success-0ap3000001103347)
- **NGS "Combine IQ" (2025):**
  - Incorporates RFID tracking of drills, college production, size and a consensus of 10 draft boards.
  - Estimates athleticism for players who do not test.
  - NGS claims the scores correlate well with becoming a starter or Pro Bowler; no validation statistics are published.
  - Source: [AP via ClickOnDetroit](https://www.clickondetroit.com/tech/2025/02/27/nfl-nextgen-stats-team-unveils-new-tool-to-help-fans-better-understand-combine-results/)
- **[PARTIAL] Sanchez, E., Weiss, L., Williams, T., Ward, P., Peterson, B., Wellman, A., Crandall, J. (2023).** "Positional Movement Demands during NFL Football Games: A 3-Year Review."
  - Data: NFL tracking 2018–2020.
  - Findings: WR and DB had the highest max velocities and the most acceleration/deceleration efforts. Linemen were lowest on every metric. LBs did the most high-velocity running.
  - The venue was given as "Performance Analysis in Sport and Exercise" by a company blog. Venue is unconfirmed.
  - Source: [NASE blog reproducing the abstract](https://www.naseinc.com/blog/positional-movement-demands-during-nfl-football-games/)
- **Big Data Bowl entry on running-back effort:** used 2022 weeks 1–9 tracking data and 69 RBs with at least 20 runs. It estimated a maximal acceleration–speed frontier as an "effort" proxy and found effort alone did not explain play success — [CMU SURE 2025 poster](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/cmsac/sure/2025/showcase/football_effort/poster.pdf)
- **Big Data Bowl entry, "Efficient speed usage and the impact of fatigue"** (Ha) — [NFL Operations PDF](https://operations.nfl.com/media/3673/big-data-bowl-calestini_ha-v2.pdf)
- **Methods for computing speed and acceleration from tracking coordinates** (soccer): Wu & Swartz (SFU) — [speed](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/speed.pdf), [acceleration](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/acceleration.pdf)
- **Individual in-situ acceleration–speed (A–S) profile** (proof of concept in professional football) — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4070585)

### Inferences
- In-game acceleration–speed profiles from tracking (maximal speed, maximal acceleration at a given speed, deceleration capacity) are the natural counterparts of the 10-yd split, the 40 and the agility drills. They are measured in context, repeatedly, and for every player, including those who skipped drills.

### Gaps
- No MIT Sloan SAC paper specifically on NFL tracking-based athleticism was found in these searches.
- The NGS athleticism-score formula and weights, and any validation metrics, are not public.
- No published study validates combine 40 or split times against in-game NGS max speed.

## Q4. Survivorship / selection bias: draft capital drives playing time. How to control for it?

### Takeaway
Draft position causally increases playing time independent of ability. Keefer's regression-discontinuity estimate is about 2.7 more starts per 10% more salary-cap value at the round 1/2 boundary. As a result, outcomes such as games played, starts or cumulative yards are contaminated by draft capital, and players who never make a roster are missing from the data.

### Cited Findings
- **[VERIFIED] Keefer, Q.A.W. (2017).** "The Sunk-Cost Fallacy in the National Football League: Salary Cap Value and Playing Time." *J Sports Economics* 18(3):282–297.
  - Method: fuzzy regression discontinuity at the round 1/2 cutoff.
  - Finding: a 10% increase in salary-cap value leads to about **2.7 additional games started**. First-round picks are no more productive but start more.
  - Sources: [MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/63649-the-sunk-cost-fallacy-in-the-national-football-league-salary-cap-value-and-playing-time); [Keefer CV](https://www.csusm.edu/economics/documents/cv/qkeefer.pdf); [JSE 18(3) contents](https://idrottsforum.org/journal-of-sports-economics-vol-18-2017-no-3/)
- **[PARTIAL] Keefer (2021, JSE).** "Did the 2011 change to NFL rookie compensation alter how sunk costs affect utilization?" Mentioned in search results; details not retrieved — [Keefer CV](https://www.csusm.edu/economics/documents/cv/qkeefer.pdf)
- **[PARTIAL] Keefer (2015), *J Labor Research*.** A related study reporting that the sunk-cost effect persists throughout the career of an average NFL player — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/jlabre/v36y2015i4p409-426.html). The attribution comes from a search summary and needs checking.
- **Draft value and outcomes:** top picks are overvalued, so draft slot partly reflects market error — [Massey & Thaler 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v59y2013i7p1479-1495.html)
- **Kuzmits & Adams 2008** used draft order, salary and games played as "performance". All three are directly tied to draft capital — [PMID 18841077](https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/openlb/publication/OLB-PM-18841077)

### Inferences
Recommended controls for any combine/tracking → performance analysis:
1. **Rate-based outcomes:** use per-snap or per-route efficiency metrics, not cumulative counts, so that playing time is not the outcome.
2. **Draft capital as a covariate:** include pick number (log or Jimmy Johnson/Massey-Thaler value curve) or rookie-contract cap value. Test whether athleticism adds predictive value *beyond* draft position (incremental validity).
3. **Discontinuities:** exploit round boundaries (Keefer-style RD), or compare players at similar picks with different athleticism.
4. **Selection into the sample:** handle players who never play with a Heckman-type selection model or inverse-probability weights for "plays ≥ N snaps". Alternatively, model a hurdle outcome (made roster / played) jointly with a conditional efficiency outcome.
5. **Within-player measures from tracking:** in-game speed and acceleration profiles are measured for anyone who plays at all, so they partly sidestep combine non-participation bias. Players who skip drills tend to be either top prospects or poor testers.
6. **Context controls:** team effects, scheme, OL/QB quality. Teramoto et al. flagged these as uncontrolled.

### Gaps
- I did not find any combine-validity paper that explicitly applies a draft-capital RD or selection correction. This is a clear methodological gap that tracking data could fill: in-game athleticism, per-snap efficiency, conditioning on draft slot.
- I found no published analysis of combine non-participants (opt-outs) and the bias they introduce.
